# Purpose

Did an online search using Claude.ai for a book to recommend as an introduction to the science of behavior (Behavior Analysis) for an undergraduate student in psychology, neuroscience, and computer science. Four books came to the top:
1. [Skinner, _Science and Human Behavior_ (1953)](../References.md#skinner-1953)
2. [Rasmussen, Clay, Pierce, & Cheney, _Behavior Analysis and Learning: A Biobehavioral Approach_, 7th ed. (2023)](../References.md#rasmussen-clay-pierce-cheney-2022)
3. [Catania, _Learning_, 5th ed. (2013)](../References.md#catania-2013)
4. [Staddon, _Adaptive Behavior and Learning_, 2nd ed. (2016)](../References.md#staddon-2016)

As of October 2026 I had never seen this book. I will give it a quick read and jot down a few notes here.

# Contents & Preface

Primarily an experimental analysis of behavior (EAB) book, with neural mechanisms, behavior-gene interactions, and epigenetics mixed in. Not until chapter 13 do they address applied behavior analysis (ABA). For me this is the perfect approach for an intro book: start with the basic building blocks, especially since this project intends to actually *implement* the underlying mechanisms and build on them.

Interesting line from the preface: "We recognize the importance of ***heterogeneous reductionism*** (Marr, 1977; [Marr & Zilio, 2013](../References.md#marr-zilio-2013)) -- the explaining of phenomena on multiple levels (e.g., neural and the behavioral) -- as creating a more complete science." 

# Chapter 1: A Science of Behavior: Perspective, History, and Assumptions

Here is something that would not have been in an intro book back in the 1970-1980s: neuroplasticity. I do remember in one of Skinner's books mention of the fact that yes there are neural processes underlying behavior, but that too little was known at the time for the neuroscience to be of much value. Quite a bit more is known now (though there is a long long way to go for a full accounting). This behavior analysis simulation project is, or will be, built upon some modeling of these neural processes, but as previously mentioned, only to the degree that they are "load bearing".

New terminology for me: immediate vs remote causation. The "immediate" refers to the internal biological/physiological processes involved in selection. The "remote" refers to the objectively observable types of actions/behaviors we usually talk about in selection.

Fairly standard discussion of respondent vs operant conditioning. Did not notice any mention of a unified principle of reinforcement: still present the two as separate processes. See [Donahoe, Crowley, Millard, and Stickney (1982)](donahoe-crowley-millard-stickney-1982).
